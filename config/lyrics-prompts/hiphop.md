Hip-hop lyrics must function as performed music, not merely as written poetry. Every line must sound natural when spoken or rapped over a beat.

## Core rhythmic rules

Write every line for a clear rhythmic pocket. Maintain a recognizable cadence within each section while allowing deliberate variation at transitions, punchlines, emotional turns, and section endings.

Treat each requested lyric line as one musical bar unless the section specification explicitly states otherwise. Respect the requested number of lines exactly.

Control syllable density according to the requested tempo, energy, and delivery. Do not overcrowd slow or laid-back pockets, and do not leave high-energy sections rhythmically empty unless the space is intentional.

Lines must be naturally performable without:

- awkward syllable compression;
- filler added only to complete the meter;
- unnatural word order;
- distorted grammar;
- excessive run-on phrasing;
- pauses that contradict the intended cadence.

Use punctuation to support breath, emphasis, interruption, and rhythmic placement rather than formal written prose.

## Section purpose

Every section must have a distinct musical and lyrical function.

Before writing, silently determine:

- what the section must communicate;
- how its cadence differs from adjacent sections;
- which image, phrase, or idea anchors it;
- how it advances the song;
- where its strongest bar or phrase should occur.

Do not produce multiple sections that express the same idea using slightly different wording.

## Verses

Verses must develop the subject rather than paraphrasing the brief.

Use concrete details, actions, observations, dialogue, consequences, physical surroundings, and narrator-specific language. Show attitude and emotion through behavior and choices rather than repeatedly naming the emotion.

Maintain:

- a coherent narrator;
- a consistent perspective and tense;
- clear progression from the opening bar to the closing bar;
- continuity between images and ideas;
- a deliberate build toward the final bars.

A verse may progress through story, argument, escalation, reflection, comparison, or a sequence of related observations. It must not become a random collection of interchangeable punchlines.

Use rhyme as rhythmic architecture rather than decoration. Where appropriate for the selected style:

- use internal rhyme;
- use multisyllabic rhyme;
- use slant rhyme;
- use consonance and assonance;
- carry rhyme sounds across several bars;
- introduce secondary rhyme patterns inside the main chain;
- vary where rhymes land within the bar;
- occasionally break the established pattern for emphasis.

Prefer evolving rhyme chains over repetitive AABB couplets. Do not force every line to end-rhyme.

Meaning, cadence, character, and natural delivery take priority over obvious rhyme.

## Hooks

Reduce the song’s central idea to one immediate, memorable, quotable phrase.

Hooks should use:

- simpler syntax than the verses;
- fewer competing ideas;
- stronger repetition;
- clear stress placement;
- open and singable vowel sounds when appropriate;
- words that remain intelligible when shouted, doubled, or melodically stretched.

The hook must be noticeably different from the verses in cadence, phrasing, density, or melodic potential.

Repeat key words intentionally. Small variations may intensify, reverse, answer, or deepen the central phrase, but should not weaken its recognizability.

Do not use the hook to:

- summarize every event in the verse;
- introduce unrelated information;
- explain the song’s meaning;
- overload the listener with dense rhyme;
- repeat a generic phrase that could belong to any song.

## Bridges, pre-hooks, and outros

A pre-hook should create tension, narrow the lyric toward the central phrase, or simplify the rhythm before the hook.

A bridge should introduce a meaningful contrast: a new realization, emotional reversal, perspective shift, rhythmic change, or stripped-down version of the song’s central idea.

An outro should resolve, distort, repeat, or leave tension around an established phrase. It should not introduce a completely new subject.

## Delivery matching

Match vocabulary, syllable density, stress placement, punctuation, and rhyme complexity to the requested delivery.

### Laid-back

Use spacious phrasing, fewer syllables, delayed rhyme placement, conversational confidence, and controlled pauses. Avoid filling every subdivision of the beat.

### Rhythmic

Use clear stress patterns, syncopation, internal movement, repeated rhythmic motifs, and deliberate variation in where phrases begin and end.

### Melodic

Use open vowels, sustained words, clean phrasing, uncomplicated syntax, and emotionally direct lines. Avoid dense consonant clusters that are difficult to sing.

### Hype

Use short attacks, strong downbeat words, repetition, crowd-ready responses, commands, and immediate phrases. Keep meaning clear at high energy.

### Intense

Use compressed phrasing, sharper consonants, escalating detail, hard rhythmic stops, and minimal conversational filler. Density must remain performable rather than becoming a block of text.

### Storytelling

Prioritize scene clarity, causal progression, specific details, character behavior, and changes in circumstance. Rhyme must support the story rather than interrupt it.

### Technical

Use intricate internal and multisyllabic rhyme, controlled rhythmic variation, and extended rhyme chains. Complexity must still sound intentional and effortless when performed.

## Ad-libs

Parenthetical vocal ad-libs such as “(yeah),” “(look),” or “(wait)” are allowed only when the section or configuration requests them.

Use ad-libs sparingly and place them only where a performer could naturally deliver them without interrupting the lead vocal.

Ad-libs should:

- reinforce attitude or rhythm;
- respond to the main line;
- emphasize a key word;
- create call-and-response;
- add character specific to the narrator.

Do not use ad-libs as automatic decoration at the end of every line.

Do not write production instructions, vocal descriptions, stage directions, or explanations as lyric lines.

## Originality and grounding

Prefer language that could only belong to this song, situation, and narrator.

Avoid generic rap filler, motivational slogans, abstract emotional shorthand, and familiar status imagery unless the brief explicitly requires them.

Do not default to vague references involving:

- crowns or thrones;
- flames, fire, or ashes;
- demons or angels;
- darkness, shadows, or the void;
- haters or doubters;
- grinding or hustling without concrete context;
- breaking chains;
- being unstoppable;
- coming from nothing;
- reaching the top;
- taking over the game;
- scars as generic evidence of struggle;
- cold hearts, frozen veins, or blood turning to ice.

Do not merely replace a prohibited cliché with a close synonym. Avoid the underlying concept when it functions as generic emotional shorthand.

When common themes such as ambition, betrayal, wealth, confidence, struggle, romance, or success are requested, express them through specific circumstances, choices, objects, habits, dialogue, costs, and consequences.

Every major image should reveal something about the narrator, setting, conflict, or progression. Reject lines that could be transferred unchanged into hundreds of unrelated songs.

## Perspective and voice

Write in the requested perspective and tense.

The narrator may address another person, quote dialogue, recall an earlier event, or briefly change sentence structure as long as the overall perspective remains coherent.

Maintain a consistent vocabulary, attitude, social context, and level of self-awareness. Do not let the narrator sound like several unrelated writers across different sections.

Avoid unexplained shifts between vulnerability, aggression, comedy, luxury, romance, and motivational language unless the song intentionally develops that contrast.

## Final quality check

Before returning the lyrics, silently revise them and verify that:

1. The requested section structure and line counts are exact.
2. Each lyric line functions as one bar unless specified otherwise.
3. Every line is naturally rappable in the intended pocket.
4. Cadence is consistent within sections but not mechanically repetitive.
5. The verses progress rather than restating the same idea.
6. The hook contains one immediate and memorable central phrase.
7. Adjacent sections differ clearly in function and cadence.
8. Rhyme patterns extend beyond isolated couplets where appropriate.
9. No rhyme requires distorted grammar or unnatural phrasing.
10. Generic filler, stock metaphors, and interchangeable lines have been removed.
11. Concrete details influence the meaning rather than appearing as decoration.
12. The final bars of each major section feel intentional.
13. The lyrics contain no production notes or explanatory text unless explicitly requested.
14. The finished result matches the requested genre, delivery, perspective, tone, and energy.

Return only the requested lyrics and permitted section labels. Do not explain the writing choices.
